A growing company often has the three tools already. WhatsApp is where customers write. A CRM, or a spreadsheet playing the role of one, is where someone is supposed to record the lead. A dashboard, or another spreadsheet, is what the founder opens on Monday. The work is the copying between them.

Connecting them is not a matter of adding a fourth tool. It is a matter of deciding which record is real, and making the other two read or update it.

## Three copies of one customer

Picture a lead that arrives as a WhatsApp message. A salesperson replies, then later types a shorter version into the CRM, then later updates a sheet so the weekly meeting has a number of open deals. By Thursday the chat says the customer is waiting on a quote, the CRM says “new”, and the sheet still shows last week’s stage.

Nobody is being careless. The tools do not share a status, so the most recent conversation wins until someone has time to reconcile. Reconciliation is the hidden job.

## Pick the record before you pick the integration

Choose one place that owns the customer and the stage. For most small teams that is the CRM, even if the CRM is still simple. WhatsApp is a channel. The dashboard is a view. Neither should be the only copy of the truth.

Write the stages in language the team already uses. New. Talking. Quote sent. Waiting. Won. Lost, with a reason. If the stages in the CRM do not match the words people type in WhatsApp, the integration will create leads that nobody recognises.

Decide the minimum fields: name, phone, what they asked for, owner, stage. Extra fields can wait. A form that demands a full profile on the first message will be skipped, and the chat will become the database again.

## What “connected” looks like on a Tuesday

A message arrives. If the phone number matches a record, the message is attached to it and the owner is notified. If it does not match, a new lead is created with the message as the note, and someone is assigned. The stage does not change by magic. It changes when a defined event happens: a quote was sent, a meeting was booked, the customer said they are not proceeding.

The dashboard reads those stages. It does not have its own. When the founder asks what is waiting, the answer is the list of records in “waiting”, not a chart that was exported on Friday.

Notifications go to the owner. They should say who wrote and what the current stage is, and they should link to the record. A notification that only says “new WhatsApp message” sends the person back to the chat, which is how the CRM goes stale.

## What not to automate

Do not let an automated reply promise a price, a delivery date or a discount. Those depend on a person who knows the exception. An acknowledgement is enough: we have the message, this is the reference, someone will reply within the hours you actually keep.

Do not close a lead because a customer went quiet. Silence is not a stage. A task for the owner is a stage you can defend.

Do not build a dashboard of vanity totals before the stages are trustworthy. A chart of “leads this month” that double-counts the chat and the CRM will train the team to ignore it.

## A small starting scope

Connect one number, one pipeline and one view: open leads by stage and owner. Run it for the enquiries you get this month. Compare the CRM with the chats at the end of the first week and fix every lead that diverged. The second week should be quieter. That is the signal to add the next automation, such as a reminder when a quote has had no reply.

Teams that start with “integrate everything” usually pause halfway, with a webhook that creates duplicate leads. Teams that start with one record and one notification get a Monday meeting that matches the chats.
